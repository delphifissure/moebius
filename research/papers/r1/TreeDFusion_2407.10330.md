###### Abstract

We introduce Tree-D Fusion, featuring the first collection of 600,000 environmentally aware, 3D simulation-ready tree models generated through Diffusion priors. Each reconstructed 3D tree model corresponds to an image from Google’s Auto Arborist Dataset, comprising street view images and associated genus labels of trees across North America. Our method distills the scores of two tree-adapted diffusion models by utilizing text prompts to specify a tree genus, thus facilitating shape reconstruction. This process involves reconstructing a 3D tree envelope filled with point markers, which are subsequently utilized to estimate the tree’s branching structure using the space colonization algorithm conditioned on a specified genus.

1 Introduction
--------------

Trees provide immense and essential value to human society and underpin diverse ecosystems worldwide[[74](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib74)]. They cool the environment, improve air quality, capture carbon dioxide, produce oxygen, and have a positive effect on human physical and mental health[[136](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib136), [67](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib67), [130](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib130), [30](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib30), [9](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib9), [13](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib13)]. The complex effect of trees on the environment has been studied for centuries. Currently, computational models that seek to understand these relationships are hindered by a lack of data. In particular, acquiring phenotypical traits such as branching angles, wood volume, crown size, and trunk width is currently infeasible at a large scale. Simulation-ready 3D reconstructed trees (i.e., data that can be used in algorithms simulating tree development) that fit real-world data would enable the estimation of useful ecological quantities at scale. For example, the overall amount of wood or carbon sequestration, and would even allow for large-scale simulations of “what-if” scenarios, such as estimating shadow cover in both leaf-on and leaf-off scenarios at different times of the day or in different seasons[[76](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib76), [115](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib115), [133](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib133)].

Existing developmental models of vegetation typically rely on manual parameter tuning[[90](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib90), [75](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib75), [65](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib65), [52](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib52)], or on a model parameter recovery that is fit to the data[[103](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib103), [81](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib81), [73](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib73)]. Digital datasets are scarce and usually available as unstructured point clouds[[91](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib91), [57](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib57)] or images[[7](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib7)]. Providing realistically reconstructed trees that fit the input data at scale is yet to be achieved, as large-scale and geographically diverse data is needed to ensure that the synthetic versions accurately reflect the morphological diversity of real trees. Despite the abundance of tree images, converting them into 3D models has been difficult due to trees’ complex shapes and self-occlusions from branches and leaves, which often hide significant portions of a tree. Thus, reconstruction methods often rely on biologically-based developmental algorithms that approximate missing parts[[51](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib51), [72](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib72)].

We introduce Tree-D Fusion, the first tree dataset of its kind and scale, containing 600,000 3D simulation-ready tree models grounded to individual real trees via street-level imagery and diffusion priors. The generation process is informed by genus-specific characteristics learned from the Auto Arborist Dataset (AAD)[[7](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib7)] through a diffusion model trained specifically on tree images and captions. The result is a 3D envelope used by a genus-conditioned developmental model[[52](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib52)] that grows into this envelope while respecting the overall shape. The output is a reconstructed simulation-ready 3D tree model with a detailed branching structure and foliage.

We use the two Diffusion priors to reconstruct real trees from AAD, covering 23 cities across North America and over 300 genus categories. Tree-D Fusion is validated against[[92](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib92), [56](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib56), [109](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib109)]. An example in[Fig.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#S0.F1 "In Tree-D Fusion: Simulation-Ready Tree Dataset from Single Images with Diffusion Priors") left demonstrates how, from a single input image, our approach generates a model that can be used to simulate the growth of that specific real-world tree over time, even simulating the growth of leaves. Our dataset, Tree-D Fusion contains 600,000 such models grounded on real-world trees, and we believe it will standardize benchmarking and advancements in forestry.

2 Related Work
--------------

Remote sensing for trees and forests. Tree and forest monitoring is an important topic in remote sensing, forestry, and ecology. Automated methods to measure canopy cover and forest height have been explored at large scales[[21](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib21), [63](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib63), [11](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib11), [29](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib29), [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib5), [49](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib49), [62](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib62)]. Recent work seeks to do, modeling at the level of individual trees instead of forests, including individual tree detection, counting, and crown delineation[[120](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib120), [104](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib104), [121](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib121), [82](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib82), [111](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib111)] and identifying the species of individual trees in both wilderness[[39](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib39), [66](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib66), [33](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib33), [32](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib32), [22](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib22), [99](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib99), [40](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib40), [82](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib82), [119](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib119)] and urban settings[[3](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib3), [105](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib105), [131](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib131), [40](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib40), [6](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib6), [7](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib7), [118](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib118), [12](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib12)]. There is also increasing interest in understanding the structure of trees at an individual level to estimate tree attributes, including height, diameter at breast height (DBH), branching structure, and shadow estimation[[45](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib45)]. In recent years, more sophisticated sensors such as airborne LiDAR have allowed for higher detail representations of individual tree shapes and structures, e.g.,[[55](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib55), [135](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib135), [98](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib98), [8](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib8)]. However, these sensor measurements are more expensive and cumbersome to collect at scale, especially at the level of individual trees. Thus, methods that allow for scalable measurement of tree geometry can significantly benefit these fields[[110](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib110)]. Our dataset addresses this concern by offering a vast collection of realistic 3D tree models. Also, our data are grounded in real-world images and exhibit the most realistic reconstruction compared to recent methods, as demonstrated in[Sec.5.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#S5.SS1 "5.1 Quantitative Results ‣ 5 Experiments and Applications ‣ Tree-D Fusion: Simulation-Ready Tree Dataset from Single Images with Diffusion Priors").

Tree modeling has been studied from a very different perspective in the computer graphics literature. The generation of 3D tree geometric and behavioral models dates back to the seminal work of Lindenmayer, who introduced parallel string rewriting systems (L-systems) to simulate cellular subdivision[[54](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib54)]. L-systems were extended by Prusinkiewicz to capture branching patterns[[85](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib85)] and later evolved[[90](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib90)] into a full mathematical formalism that captures continuous development[[87](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib87), [86](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib86)], plant signaling[[88](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib88)], environmental interaction[[89](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib89)], and ecosystems[[26](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib26)]. Recent approaches focus on the simulation of interactions of tree models with wind[[80](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib80)], fire[[37](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib37)], climbing trees[[38](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib38)], and ecosystems[[73](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib73), [65](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib65)]. One of the most important open problems in tree modeling is reconstructing the developmental model from data[[50](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib50), [34](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib34), [102](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib102), [68](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib68)]. A developmental model that simulates an existing tree would allow for estimating its shape under different conditions and predicting its shape changes and the effect on the environment.

Tree geometry reconstruction methods can be classified according to the input data type, and the most prevailing are RGB images and point clouds. Tree reconstruction from point clouds involves creating a tree skeleton[[14](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib14)] that captures tree topology and basic features (branching angles, branch length)[[126](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib126), [61](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib61), [27](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib27)]. Other methods use voxels to retrieve the shape from particle flow[[72](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib72)] or fitting geometric proxies to approximate the branch shape[[42](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib42), [36](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib36), [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib1), [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib2)]. Although tree geometry has been extensively investigated, only a limited number of studies have concentrated on foliage reconstruction[[10](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib10), [4](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib4), [129](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib129), [17](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib17), [15](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib15)] or trees incorporating leaves[[123](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib123), [60](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib60)]. RGB images are a much more widely accessible modality, so there is significant interest in reconstructing trees from RGB image input. Some methods make use of incomplete information supplied via user input to identify the trunk and branches[[108](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib108), [107](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib107), [93](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib93), [19](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib19), [59](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib59)]. Other methods create 3D voxels with approximate information that control tree growth[[96](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib96)] or use multi-image input[[35](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib35)]. Recent deep learning approaches use transformer-based tree reconstruction to L-systems[[50](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib50)], single image-based tree reconstruction using convolutional networks[[51](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib51)], statistical inference[[44](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib44)], or adversarial networks[[59](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib59)]. Our approach is inspired by an algorithm that generates a 3D geometric envelope completed by a developmental model[[51](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib51)]. Rather than a static proxy, we offer a detailed 3D envelope capturing the tree’s shape more accurately.

Single image 3D model reconstruction has been an active area of research for a long time[[23](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib23), [114](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib114), [64](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib64), [69](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib69), [18](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib18), [79](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib79), [71](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib71), [77](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib77), [28](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib28)]. Given RGB images, these methods aim to reconstruct common 3D model types such as mesh[[114](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib114), [127](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib127), [122](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib122)], voxels[[20](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib20), [128](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib128), [124](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib124), [112](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib112), [78](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib78)], or point clouds[[31](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib31)]. Recently, diffusion models[[97](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib97)] for 3D generation applications[[113](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib113), [84](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib84), [116](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib116)] have been used as priors for single image reconstruction[[125](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib125), [25](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib25), [95](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib95), [92](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib92), [56](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib56), [109](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib109)]. These approaches significantly improve reconstruction quality for occluded parts in a given input image.

Despite these advancements, reconstructing trees from a single image remains challenging, and existing methods fail to create realistic 3D models of trees due to the intricate complexity of a tree’s structure.

Fig.[2](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ Tree-D Fusion: Simulation-Ready Tree Dataset from Single Images with Diffusion Priors") demonstrates reconstructed trees using Magic123[[92](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib92)] that generates unrealistic tree geometry. These shortcomings of existing methods can be traced back to the inadequate representation of tree geometry in the diffusion models that are used as priors for the 3D shape, and that is the problem we aim to solve. Tree-D Fusion addresses the challenge of predicting occluded branches from a single image by outlining the tree’s contours and then using a species-specific procedural model[[52](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib52)] to grow the tree within these boundaries.

4 Simulation Ready Tree Dataset
-------------------------------

We construct a large dataset of 600,000 reconstructed 3D trees that faithfully represent the 3D geometry and can simulate their growth, including environmental response to light and obstacles (see Fig.[1](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Tree-D Fusion: Simulation-Ready Tree Dataset from Single Images with Diffusion Priors")).

The release of the AAD[[7](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib7)], featuring extensive tree images from Google Street View, aids the creation of 3D tree models from single images using a developmental model, capturing a specific instance of the tree’s growth. We leverage recent advances in diffusion models to reconstruct 3D trees. Given an input image, we first reconstruct a complete 3D volume of the tree using diffusion models as priors and fit the 3D volume by a developmental model conditioned on the tree genus. In particular, we use the space colonization model[[75](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib75)], also used to reconstruct 3D tree geometry[[52](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib52)] for realistic tree growth.

An overview of our approach is illustrated in Fig.[3](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3 Background ‣ Tree-D Fusion: Simulation-Ready Tree Dataset from Single Images with Diffusion Priors"). In the following, we describe how we train tree-specific diffusion models to be used as priors in Sec.[4.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#S4.SS2 "4.2 Training Tree-Specific 2D and 3D Diffusion Prior ‣ 4 Simulation Ready Tree Dataset ‣ Tree-D Fusion: Simulation-Ready Tree Dataset from Single Images with Diffusion Priors"), followed by the details of the 3D trees reconstruction in Sec.[4.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#S4.SS3 "4.3 Single Image Tree Shape Reconstruction ‣ 4 Simulation Ready Tree Dataset ‣ Tree-D Fusion: Simulation-Ready Tree Dataset from Single Images with Diffusion Priors").

### 4.2 Training Tree-Specific 2D and 3D Diffusion Prior

Tree specific 2D prior. In order to obtain an effective 2D prior, we need a diffusion model P 𝟸⁢𝙳⁢(𝑰|𝒄;σ)subscript 𝑃 2 𝙳 conditional 𝑰 𝒄 𝜎 P_{\tt 2D}({\bm{I}}|{\bm{c}};\sigma)italic_P start_POSTSUBSCRIPT typewriter_2 typewriter_D end_POSTSUBSCRIPT ( bold_italic_I | bold_italic_c ; italic_σ ) that is trained to generate tree images 𝑰 𝑰{\bm{I}}bold_italic_I given a corresponding text caption 𝒄 𝒄{\bm{c}}bold_italic_c. We utilize the AAD[[7](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib7)], which includes around one million real tree images.

When training with these noisy labels, we observe that the diffusion model generalizes poorly and does not generate high-quality tree images. The dataset also contains images with heavy light exposure, extensive blurry images resulting from privacy filters, and low-image quality of distant trees (see Fig.[7](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#S4.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ 4.1 Application ‣ 4 Simulation Ready Tree Dataset ‣ Tree-D Fusion: Simulation-Ready Tree Dataset from Single Images with Diffusion Priors")). We semi-manually removed noisy images from the AAD to train this diffusion model. First, we divide the image into patches and measure the variance of the Laplacian in each patch. This approach measures image sharpness, averaging these values to exclude very blurry images for manual review. We will release this data along with this manuscript.

We train a tree-genus-conditioned diffusion model using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)[[41](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib41)] on top of the pre-trained Stable Diffusion 1.5 version[[97](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib97)] as LoRA[[41](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib41)] is more data efficient. We create image captions using BLIP[[53](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib53)] for genus conditioning and prefix the word “tree” within the caption with the specific genus annotation from AAD. For example, BLIP outputs the caption of ‘‘A tree next to a house.’’. The caption will be A {genus} tree next to a house, with {genus} replaced by the name of the genus.

Tree specific 3D prior. To train the 3D prior, P 𝟹⁢𝙳⁢(𝑰 π|𝑰,π;σ)subscript 𝑃 3 𝙳 conditional subscript 𝑰 𝜋 𝑰 𝜋 𝜎 P_{\tt 3D}({\bm{I}}_{\pi}|{\bm{I}},\pi;\sigma)italic_P start_POSTSUBSCRIPT typewriter_3 typewriter_D end_POSTSUBSCRIPT ( bold_italic_I start_POSTSUBSCRIPT italic_π end_POSTSUBSCRIPT | bold_italic_I , italic_π ; italic_σ ), we need data that contains paired images (𝑰,𝑰 π)𝑰 subscript 𝑰 𝜋({\bm{I}},{\bm{I}}_{\pi})( bold_italic_I , bold_italic_I start_POSTSUBSCRIPT italic_π end_POSTSUBSCRIPT ) of the same tree at two different viewing angles, and their corresponding viewing angle π 𝜋\pi italic_π.

To obtain such data, we generated synthetic 3D tree models following Lee et al. For training this 3D prior, we use tree geometry information, which includes 12 rendered views, each 30 degrees apart, per tree 3D object.

### 4.4 3D Simulation-Ready Tree Reconstruction

![Image 14: Refer to caption](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1/x8.png)

After computing the tree’s 3D geometry, we extract its 3D volume and use existing methods to create a simulation-ready 3D tree. This algorithm is parameterized to generate the tree model. While various developmental models of trees (e.g., [[103](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib103), [90](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib90)]) could be used, these are not suited for fitting the tree into a particular shape. Instead, we use the realistic tree growth algorithm[[52](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib52)] that extends the previous space colonization algorithm[[75](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib75)] for fitting the tree shape. The algorithm[[52](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib52)] is conditioned on tree genus, which prescribes branching angles, internode lengths, branch radii, and other parameters[[24](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib24), [117](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib117)]. Environmental sensitivity is simulated by following existing works[[89](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib89), [81](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib81)]. Fig.[5](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.1 Application ‣ 4 Simulation Ready Tree Dataset ‣ Tree-D Fusion: Simulation-Ready Tree Dataset from Single Images with Diffusion Priors") shows a reconstructed tree under varying conditions and Fig.[9](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#S4.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ 4.4 3D Simulation-Ready Tree Reconstruction ‣ 4 Simulation Ready Tree Dataset ‣ Tree-D Fusion: Simulation-Ready Tree Dataset from Single Images with Diffusion Priors") shows the simulation of the tree over time.

With the reconstructed 3D tree models from the Auto Arboist Datset[[7](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib7)], we aim to share the first real-world large 3D and 2D tree dataset. Our dataset can standardize benchmarking in forestry deep learning, similar to ImageNet’s impact on computer vision. This will advance research, aid in studying inaccessible trees, manage urban heat islands, and support landscape design and forestry management. Additionally, it can enhance map and augmented reality applications by displaying 3D trees.

Implementation. We implemented our interactive procedural vegetation framework with C++ and Vulkan. We also added a path tracer based on Nvidia OptiX 8.0. Using the 3D synthetic tree models, training the 3D prior model using Zero123[[56](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib56)] took 89 hours using 6 NVIDIA RTX5000 GPUs, and training the 2D prior model using LoRA[[41](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib41)] took 9 hours using 4 NVIDIA RTX5000 GPU devices on the San Jose dataset. Reconstructing a 3D tree model from an RGB image takes 20 minutes using a single NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU, and it would require ≈\approx≈23 GPU-years on the single GPU to complete our 600,000 3D tree dataset.

Experiment setup. We conduct experiments using the AAD dataset[[7](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib7)] that contains 23 cities; among these, we use the city of San Jose to benchmark our system. We select the five most common tree genera, Cupressus, Magnolia, Pinus, Ligustrum, and Cinnamomum, from the city.

Baselines. We compare with the Radial Bounding Volume (RBV)[[51](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib51)], a tree-specific reconstruction using a single image with Deep Learning. We also provide comparisons with recent state-of-the-art single-image 3D object reconstruction methods based on diffusion models, including Zero123[[56](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib56)], Magic123[[92](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib92)], DreamGaussian[[109](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib109)].

Evaluation metrics. We evaluate our generated 3D tree models from the San Jose subset of AAD, which contains 4,400 trees with 133 genus-level categories. We use four evaluation metrics:

1.   1.ICTree[[83](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib83)] evaluates tree plausibility, focusing on their unique traits. It assesses various geometric aspects, including branch straightness and segment ratios, using 29 parameters to analyze branching structures, as discussed in works[[50](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib50), [134](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib134)]. 
2.   2.LPIPS[[132](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib132)] measures image similarity between the rendered front view of the reconstructed tree model (3D tree envelope) and the input image. 
3.   3.CLIP-Similarity[[94](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib94)] measures the similarity between images at a semantic level. We compute the average CLIP similarity between the input image and four renderings of the reconstructed tree at orientations 0 o, 90 o, 180 o, and 270 o. Higher values indicate the renderings are more similar to the input. 
4.   4.Chamfer distance measures the differences in an overall tree structure using lidar scanned tree point cloud data[[58](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib58)] and sampled point clouds from our generated 3D tree models.

Based on the four reconstruction metrics, Tree-D Fusion shows it becomes a new state-of-the-art method to reconstruct a 3D tree model from a single unposed RGB image. Thus, our dataset contains 3D tree models that are perpetually, visually, and geometrically realistic.

### 5.2 Qualitative Results

We show the existing methods generate unsatisfying tree structures from a single image due to a lack of tree geometry information and visualize its front(Fig.[10](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#S5.F10 "Figure 10 ‣ 5 Experiments and Applications ‣ Tree-D Fusion: Simulation-Ready Tree Dataset from Single Images with Diffusion Priors")) and top-views(Fig.[11](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#S5.F11 "Figure 11 ‣ 5 Experiments and Applications ‣ Tree-D Fusion: Simulation-Ready Tree Dataset from Single Images with Diffusion Priors")). RBV[[51](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib51)] capture the approximated contour estimation, Zero123[[56](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib56)] also captures contour information but includes holes in the Ginko and Ligustrum genus, and the front-view is flat. Also, it generates non-tree-like shapes near the top area in the Tristaniopsis tree genus. Magic123[[92](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib92)] fails to estimate tree depth, concentrating on the center and creating a gap between the inner and outer sides. Moreover, it does not generate overall balanced tree volumes and tends to look overly simplistic. DreamGaussian[[109](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib109)] captures the overall tree contour but it creates numerous holes around the center of 3D tree models with bubble-like artifacts visible from the top view. Tree-D Fusion captures the tree contours without any holes and generates tree-like balanced volumetric trees from the front and the top view.

6 Conclusion
------------

We introduce Tree-D Fusion, the first large dataset of 3D reconstructed tree models enabled by a diffusion-based approach to construct 3D tree models from single images. We use images from the AAD[[7](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#bib.bib7)] and synthetic trees to train diffusion models that reconstruct 3D tree volumes that are then completed by a developmental model to provide functional-structural tree model for individual real trees. Our generated dataset has the potential to enable diverse and impactful applications in 3D tree phenotyping at a previously unprecedented scale.

![Image 76: Refer to caption](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1/x10.png)

Figure 13: Single-image reconstruction fails with asymmetric trees, producing different 3D models from varying views.

This is the first large dataset of its kind, but it comes with a few limitations. First, the 3D model shapes are constrained by the capabilities of the simulator. Second, single-image reconstruction, limited by a single viewpoint and missing information, is best suited for symmetric trees. An example in Fig.[13](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.10330v1#S6.F13 "Figure 13 ‣ 6 Conclusion ‣ Tree-D Fusion: Simulation-Ready Tree Dataset from Single Images with Diffusion Priors") epitomizes this case. The same asymmetric tree from two different angles yields significantly varied 3D models. High branch density can also hinder precise reconstruction due to the complexity of tree segmentation. Finally, predicting a branch structure obscured by leaves with 100% fidelity to nature is an ongoing challenge due to its complex geometry.

There are many possible avenues for future work. First, a comprehensive 3D tree database could enable the study of light and environmental effects on reconstruction. Additionally, incorporating real tree models with algorithms for partially occluded parts during training could improve detection significantly, as single-image segmentations struggle to disentangle nearby tree crowns.
